Monty Finance

| STAGE: RUNNING | STARTED: AUGUST 2026 |

I built a script that runs every evening, simulates trades across the stock and crypto markets, and uses machine learning to evolve its own strategies over time. It manages eleven competing strategies, decides on its own what to change, and emails me what it did and why.

It trades paper money. The point is not to make money yet, it is to find out whether any of this works when measured honestly.

What it does

Every evening, without me:

It pulls the day's closing prices, runs eleven separate strategies against them, and records the trades each one would have made. It checks how much risk each strategy is carrying and pulls back the ones that are struggling. It considers a few changes it could make to improve itself, tests each one against data it has not seen, and applies at most one if the test says it genuinely helps. Then it sends me a report with charts.

Ten of the eleven strategies are rules I wrote. The eleventh is different: its rules were discovered by a genetic algorithm that breeds strategies against each other, keeps the ones that survive, and rewrites the loser. The real question the project is asking is whether that evolved strategy can beat the ten a human designed, with all eleven starting from the same money.

I am not in the loop. It decides, and I read what it did afterward. Any change it makes I can undo with one command.

The hard part

Almost every trading system you find online shows a beautiful chart of what it would have earned. Almost none of them mention that such a chart is the easiest thing in finance to manufacture. If you test enough strategies against past prices, one of them will look brilliant purely by chance, and it will stop working the moment it meets a market it has not seen.

So the interesting engineering problem here is not building a strategy. It is building a system that can look at its own best result and say "this is probably luck."

Most of Monty Finance exists to do exactly that. It tests every strategy against the worst market it can find rather than the average one. It keeps a slice of recent data sealed off and never looks at it during the search. And it mathematically discounts every result by how many things it tried, because the more strategies you test, the better the luckiest one looks.

The first serious run produced a strategy that looked good and was rejected anyway, because that discount showed it was indistinguishable from chance. That rejection is the most useful thing the system has produced so far.

What I am testing

It went live in August 2026 with eleven simulated funds of $1,000 each and a simulated $100 a month going into every one. It is measured against the S&P 500 over a multi-year horizon.

My honest expectation is that most of the eleven will lose to simply buying an index fund. That would be a finding, not a failure. In an earlier one-year simulation the system earned $159 of profit against $3,150 of contributions, which is to say the deposits did about 95% of the work. At small account sizes, saving consistently beats being clever by an enormous margin, and it was worth building something honest enough to show me that.

Under the hood

The detail, for anyone who wants it.

Structure. Eleven independent funds, each holding 80% equities and 20% crypto. Each sizes positions through four risk controls that compound: fractional Kelly sizing from its own realized edge, a drawdown circuit breaker that halves exposure at -12% and stops trading at -22%, a volatility brake, and a high-water ratchet that protects gains as they accumulate. All performance is time-weighted, so contributions never masquerade as returns.

What is genuinely machine learning. The genetic algorithm: a population of 200 candidate strategies, tournament selection, crossover that swaps rule blocks between parents, mutation, and speciation by genetic distance so the population does not collapse onto a single idea. Everything else is not learning, and I would rather say so. The loop that tunes parameters is hill-climbing with an out-of-sample gate. The risk layer is closed-form math. There are no neural networks, because daily prices give roughly 250 samples a year and that is far too thin to train one honestly.

The statistics. Evaluate 8,000 candidate strategies and the best result drawn from pure random noise reaches an annualized Sharpe ratio near 3.0. A strategy scoring 1.3 after that search has therefore found nothing, even though 1.3 would be respectable if you had only tried once. The system computes a deflated Sharpe ratio that corrects for the number of trials and refuses to call anything validated below a 95% probability of genuine skill.

What it cannot do. The stock list is today's tickers, so companies that failed are underrepresented and every backtest is flattered by survivorship bias. The strategies were designed by someone who lived through the markets they are tested on. Fills are simulated at closing prices with flat costs rather than real spreads. No shorting, no options, no leverage, deliberately.

Stack

Python, pandas, NumPy, SQLite, matplotlib, and a local language model for writing the daily summary. No cloud and no external services. The whole thing runs on a MacBook Pro and finishes its nightly work in under a minute.
